# Everyone in industrial AI now says the same six things

I read one month of everything published and discussed about connected worker platforms, tribal knowledge and AI on the shop floor. The vocabulary has converged almost completely. The proof has not. That gap is the whole opportunity.

## The demand driver is demographic, not technological

Start with the thing that actually makes a plant manager pick up the phone.

A quarter of the manufacturing workforce is over 55, up from 14% in 2000. 26% are expected to retire by 2030, leaving roughly 1,5 million roles unfilled. 75% of manufacturers report a skill deficit. 97% say they are concerned about the brain drain.

Frost and Sullivan lists the three drivers of this category as labour shortages, aging workforce demographics and increasing operational complexity. Two of the three are demographic.

This matters because it sets the clock. Software adoption in manufacturing is usually slow because the cost of not adopting is diffuse. Here it is not diffuse. It is a named person with a retirement date, and everything they know that nobody wrote down leaves the building on that date.

## The loudest number in the category is now the failure rate

The second thing that changed is what people quote at each other.

Deloitte found that 87% of manufacturers have started a generative AI pilot and 24% have reached facility-level adoption. IDC puts it at 4 of every 33 pilots reaching production. Gartner simultaneously predicts that 40% of enterprise applications will carry task-specific AI agents by 2026, and that more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027.

Two years ago the shareable statistic in this space was a promise. Now it is a failure rate. Anyone pitching into a factory in 2026 is walking into a room where the objection has already been made, by an analyst, in writing.

That is not a reason to be quieter about what software can do. It is a reason to lead with what happens after the pilot.

## The phrasebook has converged, and that is a problem for everyone in it

Read six vendor sites in a row and the effect is genuinely disorienting.

The independent comparison roundups name the same set every time: Poka for tribal knowledge capture, Augmentir for AI skills management, Tulip for no-code apps, Parsable for SOPs with conditional logic, Innovapptive for AI-agent frontline execution, SafetyCulture for inspection compliance. Six names, and a shared script.

A connected worker demo published this month opens on "disconnected paper records, slow, fragile, and impossible to track in real time" and closes on "clear instructions, confident actions, all in one intuitive digital platform". You could put almost any logo on it.

There is also a three-way vocabulary split underneath the sameness. Gartner writes Augmented Connected Workforce. Frost and Sullivan writes Augmented Connected Worker. Buyers and comparison sites write connected worker platform. And the phrase people actually search for is tribal knowledge, which has no analyst behind it at all, just plant managers describing their problem in their own words.

When the words are interchangeable, wording cannot differentiate. Only proof can. Named plants, real numbers, a specific failure mode that was solved and how long it took.

## The argument that survives is "inside the work"

There is one claim in the category that is doing real work right now, and it is a claim about placement rather than capability.

Tulip frames it as troubleshooting at the station, quality decisions in the workflow, operator guidance in context, documentation captured as the process happens. The same company publishes the figure that 81% of manufacturing task hours are expected to remain human-driven.

Put those together and you get the sharpest available test for any tool aimed at a shop floor: does it require someone to change their day in order to work?

A dashboard requires someone to open it. A knowledge base requires someone to fill it. A model that produces an insight requires someone to be assigned to act on it. Each of those is a behaviour change asked of people who are already fully occupied. That is what the 87 to 24 gap is measuring.

Capture that happens during work does not need a behaviour change. It needs to fit in twenty seconds and speak the operator's language.

## The audience is AI-suspicious, and they are right to be

The single most engaged industrial thread I found in the whole window had nothing to do with platforms.

A machine shop owner in Japan had been posting his work. Enough people assumed the posts were AI-generated that he filmed himself in the shop at the wire EDM that cuts his lantern cages, with subtitles, to prove he was a person. It drew 2.359 upvotes.

Asked about the machine, he said: "Good eye. Yes, it is an older Mitsubishi. We put money into maintenance every year to keep it running, so it still holds tolerance and works every day. No reason to replace it."

Both halves of that are instructive. This audience reads polish as a warning sign. And it judges equipment on whether it holds tolerance and works every day, not on whether it is new. Anything you put in front of them gets measured against a machine that has earned its place over decades.

## What to do with this if you are buying

Six things everyone says. Here is what I would actually ask a vendor.

Ask what happens in the twenty seconds after an operator notices something. If the answer involves opening an application, finding a form and typing, the tool will be used for three weeks.

Ask for a plant, not a logo. The category's own statistics say most pilots do not reach production, so ask to speak to one that did.

Ask what it captures from the 81% of work that stays human. Not what it automates. What it learns from the people who are still there.

And ask them to say what they do without using the words connected, seamless or unified. If nothing is left, you have learned something useful.
