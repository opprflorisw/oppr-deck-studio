87% of manufacturers have started a generative AI pilot. 24% have one running at facility level.

That gap is the most honest number in industrial AI right now, and it is the reason your operations director is tired.

IDC puts it more bluntly: for every 33 AI pilots launched, 4 reach production. Gartner expects more than 40% of agentic AI projects to be cancelled by the end of 2027, on cost, unclear business value or weak risk controls.

I do not think this is an argument against AI on the shop floor. I think it is an argument about where you put it.

The pilots that die are the ones that live next to the work. A dashboard someone has to remember to open. A knowledge base someone has to remember to fill. A model that produces an insight nobody was asked to act on. Every one of those depends on a behaviour change from people who are already fully occupied running a line.

The ones that survive show up inside the work. Troubleshooting at the station. A quality decision inside the task that was going to happen anyway. Documentation captured as the process runs, not as a separate job at the end of the shift.

Tulip's own framing of this is worth borrowing: 81% of manufacturing task hours are expected to stay human-driven. So the useful question is not what you can automate away. It is what you can capture from the 81% that is still there.

If your pilot needs someone to change their day to work, it is already in the 76%.
